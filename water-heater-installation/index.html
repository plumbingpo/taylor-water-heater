A homeowner wants the heater out of a hall closet and into the garage or basement. A remodel calls for a second unit at the far end of the house, or an all-electric conversion means trading gas for a heat pump model. Every one of them involves new work: gas lines sized for the load, vents routed and pitched properly, circuits matched to the heater, and water lines run cleanly. Taylor Water Heater works all of that out before the installation starts.

Sizing and placement shape most of the outcome. For a tank, the first-hour rating is the number we compare against your household's busiest hour; for a tankless unit, we size by flow rate and winter temperature rise. Then comes placement. Gas heaters need a proper vent route and combustion air, heat pump heaters need open space and a condensate drain, and whole-house electric tankless units often need a large dedicated circuit and the panel capacity to match. Any heater placed above a finished ceiling needs a drain pan, and a gas unit in a garage may need to sit on a raised platform where code requires it. You get the trade-offs in plain terms, with no pressure.

On installation day, every step gets verified. We run and pressure-test new gas piping, add a sediment trap, and route venting exactly to spec. For electric and heat pump units, the circuit is matched to the load. Water lines get accessible shutoffs and proper metal transitions, and the relief valve discharge runs full size to a safe point. Where code requires an expansion tank, drain pan, or seismic straps, we add them, and we pull the permit when your jurisdiction needs one. Warning Signs to Watch For

Water heaters usually give plenty of warning before they fail, as long as you know what to look and listen for. These are the red flags our technicians see most often before a breakdown:

  • A remodel that moves the utility space to a new spot
  • Missing code items like an expansion tank, drain pan, or proper venting
  • An addition too far from the existing heater for quick hot water
  • A large soaking tub that a standard tank cannot fill
  • A heater squeezed into a closet or attic where a leak would do real damage
  • Long waits for hot water at faucets far from the heater
  • Plans to change fuel types or go all-electric
  • Hot water running out before everyone has showered

What a Trained Technician Brings to the Job

Installing a new water heater means doing several trades' work at once. Gas piping has to be sized for the heater's input and every other appliance on the line, then pressure-tested and leak-free. The vent must meet the manufacturer's rules on diameter, length, pitch, and clearances, or carbon monoxide can find its way indoors. Electric and heat pump units need the right circuit, breaker, and wire gauge, and a whole-house electric tankless unit can demand more current than many panels can spare. On the water side, there is more: shutoffs, dielectric transitions, a correctly rated relief valve with a full-size discharge line, and code items like expansion tanks, drain pans, and seismic straps that vary by location.

With a professional install, every piece is done right once. Our experienced technicians size the heater, calculate the gas line, lay out the vent route, confirm electrical capacity, and add the code items your location calls for. We test for leaks, check draft and carbon monoxide on gas units, and leave the heater at a safe temperature with the paperwork in order. How to Prevent Water Heater Problems

A little routine care goes a long way toward keeping a water heater running well past its expected lifespan. Here's where to begin:

  • Keep a clear path to the heater so it can be serviced and inspected easily
  • Plan an annual descaling if you installed a tankless unit
  • Flush a tank every six to twelve months to keep sediment from settling
  • Set the thermostat near 120 degrees for safety and efficiency
  • Keep flammable liquids and clutter away from gas heaters
  • Have the anode rod inspected every few years to protect the tank
  • Moisture in the drain pan or at the relief line is an early warning worth a call
