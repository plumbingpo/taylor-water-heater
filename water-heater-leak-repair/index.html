A leaking water heater is not automatically a heater at the end of its life. Plenty of leaks come from parts that are cheap and quick to replace: a relief valve that no longer seats, a plastic drain valve that has started to weep, a supply connector with a tired gasket, or a corroded fitting at the top of the tank. Some puddles are not leaks at all but condensation, especially on a newly filled tank or a gas unit starting up in muggy weather. The one leak that cannot be repaired is water seeping from the steel tank, usually through the bottom or a seam. For Taylor, MI homeowners, the difference between a small repair and a new heater is why diagnosis comes first.

A leak call with us starts with evidence, not guesses. The technician wipes the tank and fittings dry so fresh water shows where it starts, then checks the relief valve and its discharge line, the drain valve at the base, and every connection on top. On gas models we also inspect the burner compartment, because water there can be normal startup condensation or a sign of a tank that has failed. A relief valve that keeps releasing water triggers a pressure test: static pressure above roughly 80 psi, or a missing expansion tank on a closed system, will keep a new valve weeping. Findings come first; the repair follows.

Most leaks are fixed in a single visit. We replace worn relief valves, swap plastic drain valves for durable brass ones, rebuild corroded connections, add or recharge an expansion tank, and install a pressure-reducing valve when street pressure runs high. If the tank itself is leaking, there is no safe patch, and you will hear that from us directly along with your replacement options. We also help prevent the next leak from doing damage: a drain pan piped to a proper drain, a water sensor or automatic shutoff valve, and a clear label on the shutoff. How to Tell Something Is Wrong

Most tanks announce trouble weeks or months ahead with rumbling noises, rusty water, or hot showers that keep getting shorter. If any of the following sound familiar, it's worth having the unit checked:

  • Water collecting under or beside the heater
  • Warped baseboards or stained drywall beside the water heater
  • Water inside the burner compartment of a gas heater
  • Higher water use with no obvious explanation
  • Brown hot water along with moisture around the tank bottom
  • Moisture or mineral crust at the end of the relief valve pipe
  • Steady drips from the drain valve near the base
  • Mineral buildup or rust around the hot and cold connections

Why This Job Calls for a Pro

Water heater leaks are easy to misread. Water pooling at the base may start at a fitting on top and run down the jacket, and condensation on a cold tank can look exactly like a failure. Buying the wrong part is money down the drain, and overlooking a tank that is starting to go can lead to a much bigger flood later. There are safety concerns, too. The relief valve protects against dangerous temperature and pressure, and capping or plugging it to stop a drip can set up a violent failure. Corroded fittings on an older tank can crack under a wrench, and electric heaters sit on 240-volt circuits that do not mix well with wet floors.

With a trained eye, the answer usually comes quickly. We isolate each possible source in turn, confirm it with fresh water on a dried surface, and measure static pressure so a new valve does not start dripping next week. Worn relief valves, weeping drain valves, and corroded connections are swapped the same visit, and pressure problems get a permanent fix, whether that is an expansion tank or a pressure-reducing valve. A rusted-through tank gets an honest replacement recommendation rather than a patch, and we can add a piped drain pan and a leak sensor to protect your home going forward.

How to Prevent Water Heater Problems

Routine maintenance is almost always cheaper and easier than an early replacement. Here's where to begin:

  • An expansion tank that is present and properly charged keeps the relief valve from opening every cycle
  • Replace a depleted anode rod before the steel tank becomes the next target for corrosion
  • Check your water pressure; if it runs above about 80 psi, a pressure-reducing valve protects the tank and valves
  • Annual flushing removes sediment that traps heat against the tank floor
  • Put a water leak sensor on the floor next to the tank so a drip gets noticed early
  • Make a habit of checking around the heater for moisture or corrosion every few weeks
  • Place the heater in a drain pan with a line to a floor drain or outdoors, especially above finished space
