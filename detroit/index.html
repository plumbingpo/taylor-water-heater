How Detroit Homeowners Can Extend Water Heater Life

A little routine care is the difference between a water heater that lasts and one that fails early. Our advice for homeowners is a short list: a yearly flush, an anode inspection every few years, a yearly test of the T&P valve, a thermostat set near 120 degrees, and annual descaling for tankless units on hard water, so problems are found while they're still inexpensive.

Age matters too. Most tank water heaters last 8 to 12 years, tankless units often reach 20 with regular descaling, and heat pump models typically run 10 to 15. If your heater is past that window and showing warning signs like discolored hot water, rumbling during heating, dampness under the tank, or hot water that runs out sooner than it used to, a planned replacement is almost always cheaper and much easier on you than an emergency one with water on the floor.

Left alone, a small problem tends to grow. Take a spent anode rod: replacing it is a quick, inexpensive repair, but once it's gone, the water starts eating the tank itself, and just like that a minor fix has become a full replacement. That's why we encourage homeowners to keep our number in their phone and reach out at the first hint of trouble. Catching it early saves money and prevents a flooded utility room.

What Goes Wrong With Local Water Heaters

The majority of water heater problems we see in Detroit start with what's in the water. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, minerals can be a real factor: calcium and magnesium drop out as sediment on the tank floor, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, which produces the popping and rumbling many homeowners hear when the heater fires. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, and that's why regular descaling matters so much in hard-water areas.

We also see a lot of aging equipment. A standard tank water heater usually runs 8 to 12 years, and by then the anode rod is often eaten away, leaving the steel open to rust. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, which means we service both sides: thermocouples, burners, and venting on gas units, and heating elements and thermostats on electric ones.

Setup matters too. A home on a private well may see more sediment and occasionally a sulfur smell in the hot water, which a different anode rod often cures. A tankless heater in hard water should get regular descaling. And an older install being replaced often needs updates that weren't required when it went in: an expansion tank, a correctly piped relief valve line, a drain pan, or strapping where required. Every one of these problems is fixable, and most are much cheaper to address early.
